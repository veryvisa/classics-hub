Chuanxilu (Instructions for Practical Living)

As a young man Wang Yangming took Zhu Xi at his word: everything has its principle, and by investigating things one at a time a person can become a sage. He and a friend sat down in front of the bamboo in the courtyard to investigate it. The friend fell ill after three days; Wang lasted seven and fell ill as well. No principle had come out of the bamboo. Twenty years later, banished to the hills of Longchang in Guizhou, he woke one night with the answer: the principle was never in the bamboo. It was in his own mind.

What kind of book this is

The Chuanxilu collects Wang Yangming's (1472–1529) conversations and letters in three parts. The first was recorded by his brother-in-law and first disciple Xu Ai and others and printed in 1518; the second consists of letters in his own hand; the third holds conversations from his last years and was completed only in 1556, after his death. The title comes from a line in the Analects about practising what has been passed on.

He was no cloistered scholar. He put down rebellions, governed provinces, was flogged at court and sent into exile. What the book records was said between campaigns, court cases and lectures, which is why it has so little patience for empty talk.

Read these lines first

His student Xu Ai objected: everyone knows they should be good to their parents and many fail to act on it, so knowing and acting are plainly two things. Wang replied that selfish desire has cut them apart, and that this is not what knowing and acting originally are. His example is hating a foul smell: smelling it is the knowing and the dislike is the acting, with no gap in which one first notes the smell and then decides to dislike it. To really know is already to be acting.

A friend pointed at flowering trees on a hillside and asked: you say nothing exists outside the mind, yet these flowers bloom and fall by themselves; what have they to do with my mind? This was the answer. He is not saying the flowers do not exist. He is saying that their colours being bright is something that happens only when someone looks. Meaning arises where mind and thing meet.

These four lines are his own late summary of his teaching. The first has always been the contentious one. His two leading disciples understood it differently on the spot: one held that even good and evil must be let go, the other that one must plainly do good and remove evil. Wang said both were right, for different kinds of people. After his death the school split along exactly that seam.

A student said he felt fine while sitting quietly and fell apart as soon as something happened. This was the reply, and it is where Wang parts from Chan: the work is done not on the meditation cushion but while handling whatever is in front of you.

The word most often misread: liangzhi

良知 liangzhi is not conscience in the everyday sense of a twinge of guilt, nor a set of moral rules one has learned. Wang means a direct perception of right and wrong that everyone is born with, as the eye tells black from white. The trouble is never that it is missing; self-interest covers it over. To "extend" it is to carry that perception through into every particular situation without discount.

The strongest objection came from Zhu Xi's side: if everyone acts on their own innate knowing, who is to tell it from mere wilfulness? In the late Ming there were indeed people who indulged themselves under its banner. Wang's answer was tempering on affairs: innate knowing is not a pleasant feeling but something tested over and over in real dealings. The argument continues today in other words: can moral intuition be trusted? The psychologist Jonathan Haidt has argued that most moral judgment is intuition first and reasoning afterwards, which supports Wang's emphasis on intuition and is also a reminder that intuitions carry bias.

How it stands with the others

Towards the Analects and Mencius, Wang saw himself as returning to the original meaning. Towards Zhu Xi he was the most important dissenter: Zhu said principle is in all things and must be sought outwardly; Wang said principle is in the mind and must be brought to light inwardly. From Chan he borrowed a great deal (the present moment, original completeness) while holding two lines: no withdrawal from human relationships and affairs, and no letting go of good and evil together; see the Platform Sutra.

His teaching reached Japan in the seventeenth century, where it was known as Yomeigaku and remained influential down to the end of the shogunate. On the question map his line stands next to the answers of Laozi, Huineng and Xunzi.

Translations

The standard complete English translation is Wing-tsit Chan's Instructions for Practical Living (1963), still in copyright. An earlier partial version by Frederick Henke appeared in 1916. The English here is our own.
