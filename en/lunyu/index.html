Confucian · Warring States

Lunyu (The Analects)

Confucius spent his life trying to win an office in which he could put his ideas into practice, and largely failed; he wandered from state to state for fourteen years and was turned away everywhere. What he left behind was not a theory but conversations noted down by his students. They ask, he answers, and he answers different people differently.

What kind of book this is

Twenty books, roughly five hundred passages, about sixteen thousand characters. Most passages are a sentence or two, with no argument. Confucius did not write it; students and students of students compiled it over the decades and generations after his death, so some layers are earlier than others, and the first ten books are generally thought closer to the original material than the last ten.

Three versions circulated in the Han: those of Lu, of Qi, and an "old text". The received Analects descends mainly from the Lu version. The Qi version had two extra books that were lost for nearly two thousand years. Among the bamboo slips from the tomb of the Marquis of Haihun at Nanchang, Jiangxi, excavated from 2011, is one headed "Knowing the Way", which scholars identify as one of those lost Qi books.

Read these lines first

The book opens with three things: the pleasure of learning itself, companions, and how to bear going unrecognised. The third is the easiest to skip and may be the one Confucius himself had to work hardest at.

Asked for one word to live by for a lifetime, Confucius answered 恕 shu, putting oneself in the other's place, and explained it with this same sentence.

This is Confucius in old age looking back. Notice where it ends: not with desire held down, but with what he wants and what is right no longer in conflict.

Two key words: ren and li

仁 ren appears more than a hundred times, and Confucius never defines it; each answer depends on who is asking. It is less an inner feeling of kindness than a kind of completeness as a person, which can only be achieved in relationships with others. "Benevolence", "humaneness", "goodness": each English rendering catches part of it.

禮 li is more than etiquette. It is the whole repertoire of forms that spare people from renegotiating every encounter: how to address an elder, how to apologise, how to see off the dead. Confucius's claim is that ren is trained through li, and li without ren is an empty shell.

How it stands with the others

The Daoists thought the Confucians meddled too much: the more benevolence is preached, the worse the times must be. The Mohists thought them partial: if everyone loves their own parents more than other people's, how can the world be at peace? The Legalists thought them naive: states run on institutions, not character. All three objections can still be heard in any argument about education or management.

More than a thousand years later, Confucians of the Song and Ming absorbed Buddhist and Daoist discussions of the mind and reread the Analects. Zhu Xi placed it first among the Four Books; Wang Yangming argued that everything Confucius taught is rooted in one's own mind, as the Chuanxilu shows. On death, which Confucius declined to discuss, the question map shows how the others took up the question.

Translations

James Legge's translation of 1861 is in the public domain. Among modern versions, D. C. Lau (1979) is spare; Roger Ames and Henry Rosemont (1998) stress relationship and process; Edward Slingerland (2003) includes selections from the traditional commentators and suits readers who want to see the disagreements.
