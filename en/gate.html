From the body into the ideas

Medicine as the gate: five ideas, five doors

The Huangdi Neijing was not written in a vacuum. Between the Warring States and the Han, yin and yang, the five phases and 氣 qi were the shared vocabulary of the educated: astronomers used it, political advisers used it, and so did physicians. That is why a reader of the medical classic keeps running into Laozi, the Changes and the Confucians, and why those abstract sayings find their first tangible examples in medicine.

Yin and yang: from the two sides of a hill to everything that comes in pairs

The two characters first meant the shady and the sunny side of a hill. By the Warring States period they had become a way of describing every pair of opposites that depend on each other: day and night, cold and heat, movement and stillness. Physicians applied it to the body: waking is yang, sleep is yin.

From here the path runs to the Changes and the Dao De Jing. One caution: yin and yang name a relationship, not two substances. The same person is yang to a child and yin to a parent. Treating them as fixed labels for good and bad, or male and female, is the commonest later misreading.

The five phases: a system of classification, and its limits

In the Great Plan the five are still just useful materials. In the third century BCE Zou Yan turned them into a cycle of mutual generation and conquest and used it to explain the rise and fall of dynasties. Han thinkers then mapped colours, directions, seasons, tastes, organs and emotions onto the same grid. When the Neijing says the liver belongs to wood and the heart to fire, it is using that grid.

Early translators wrote "five elements", which invites comparison with the four elements of the Greeks. But 行 xing means "to go": these are five phases of change, and "five phases" is now the usual rendering. As a tool for memory and association the grid works well. As an explanation of nature it was doubted by Chinese thinkers themselves; Wang Chong picked it apart in the first century CE. The medical manuscripts found at Mawangdui in 1973 are older than the Neijing, and in them the vessels are not yet tied into a tidy system of phases and organs, which shows the grid was assembled over time.

Body and cosmos: the person as a small world

When physicians say rise early and walk in spring, sleep early and rise late in winter, a Daoist view lies underneath: people are part of nature, and going with it costs less than going against it. Zhuangzi pushes the thought to its limit and says the whole world is one 氣 qi gathering and dispersing.

Not every ancient thinker agreed. Xunzi flatly refused to tie the heavens to human affairs: nature keeps its own regularities and does not change them for anyone's virtue or vice. And when Dong Zhongshu in the Han spoke of heaven "responding" to rulers with omens and disasters, that was political theory, not the same thing as the physicians' talk of seasons and the body.

Treating what is not yet ill: act while things are small

The sentence speaks of medicine and government in one breath, and that is no accident. It is close to a rewriting of a line in Laozi, and the Changes gives the same warning.

The ancients paired the good physician with the good minister: "the best physician heals the state". Preventive medicine and risk management now use a different vocabulary for the same point: problems are cheapest to handle while they are small.

Balance and harmony: not compromise, but the right measure

The physician's aim is not to wipe something out but to bring back what has tipped too far. That idea of an even level is what the Confucians call centredness and harmony.

Notice that the Mean does not tell anyone to have no feelings. It asks whether a feeling, once expressed, fits the occasion. The medical classic brings this down to the body, warning that excessive anger, joy or brooding each harm a particular organ: again the point is excess. From here the path runs to the Analects and its remark that too much is as bad as too little.

Once through the gate

The medical classics borrowed from the philosophers, but their purposes differ. Physicians wanted patients to recover and used whatever worked; the philosophers were arguing over how a person ought to live. Take the intuitions you gained from the body into the question map, and you will find the schools disagree far more than the medical texts let on.
