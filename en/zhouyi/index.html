First of the classics · Western Zhou to Han

Zhouyi (The Book of Changes)

Three thousand years ago the Zhou people consulted an oracle before any great undertaking: shall we march, shall we move the capital, shall this marriage go ahead? The diviner counted out yarrow stalks to arrive at a figure of six lines and then looked up the matching sentence in a handbook. That handbook became the book Chinese scholars valued above all others.

Two books laid one on the other

What is now called the Changes has two layers. The lower one, the core text, consists of sixty-four hexagrams, each with one statement for the whole figure and one for each of its six lines; it dates from the Western Zhou, its language is archaic, and its concern is good and bad fortune. The upper layer is ten commentaries, the "Ten Wings", written between the Warring States and the early Han. Tradition credited them to Confucius; they are now generally attributed to later Confucian scholars. The commentaries read the oracle's sentences as statements about the cosmos and human life.

Nearly all the famous lines come from the commentaries, not the core text. Telling the two layers apart is the first step in reading the Changes.

Read these lines first

The first two hexagrams, one firm and one yielding, are often read together as the two sides a person needs: to keep pressing on oneself, and to be able to bear others up.

These two lines are the heart of the book: the world is not a set of fixed things but a continual process of coming to be, and wisdom lies not in resisting change but in seeing what stage it has reached.

What the excavated manuscripts changed

The silk copy of the Changes found at Mawangdui in 1973 arranges the hexagrams in an entirely different order from the received text and carries commentaries not found there. The Chu bamboo-slip copy acquired by the Shanghai Museum in 1994, dated to about 300 BCE, is the earliest known. On still earlier oracle bones and bronzes of the Shang and Zhou, scholars have found hexagrams written as numbers, which shows that the figures began as counted numerals and that drawing them as solid and broken lines came later.

How to regard divination today

The Changes really is a book of divination, and there is no need to be coy about it. But from the Warring States on there was another way of reading it. Xunzi said that those good at the Changes do not divine. The images and statements were treated as tools for thinking about one's situation: what stage am I at, and is this a time to advance or to wait?

This site follows the second road and treats the book as intellectual history and as a way of looking at change. As for the fortune-telling and fengshui later derived from it, we describe their place in cultural history only; we make no predictions and teach no one to make them.

How it stands with the others

The Confucians ranked it first among the Five Classics, and the Daoists claimed it too: in the Wei and Jin it was grouped with the Laozi and Zhuangzi as the "three mysteries". The physicians' theory of yin and yang grows from the same root; see the gate. Nearly every Song Neo-Confucian wrote a commentary on it, using it to answer cosmological questions raised by Buddhism. And at the end of the seventeenth century Leibniz, shown a diagram of the sixty-four hexagrams by a missionary, recognised in its sequence the binary arithmetic he had just devised.

Translations

James Legge's translation of 1882 is in the public domain. The most influential version is Richard Wilhelm's German translation, put into English by Cary Baynes (1950); it carries its own interpretive tradition and is still in copyright. Translations grounded in excavated texts and modern scholarship include Richard John Lynn (1994, with Wang Bi's commentary) and Edward Shaughnessy (1996, the Mawangdui text).
