How does a restless mind settle?

Awake at three, and the mind will not stop

The oldest collection of Chinese poetry opens with a man who cannot sleep. He is in love, he is getting nowhere, he thinks of her by day and by night, and he turns over and over in bed.

Three thousand years on, the cause is a mortgage, a performance review, a child's school, an unanswered email, and the movements in bed are exactly the same. The poem has already named half the problem: what keeps him awake is not the girl but the seeking.

1What each school says

The physicians treat it first as a bodily matter. By day a person's active, defensive energy circulates at the surface and one is awake; at night it withdraws inward and one sleeps. Insomnia is that energy failing to go home when it should.

Why does it fail to go home? The classic gives specific causes, and one of them still sounds familiar: the wrong dinner.

The Daoists leave the physiology aside and talk about the direction of effort. For Laozi the mind is naturally still, like a basin of water that clears by itself if you stop stirring.

When Zhuangzi describes the people he thinks have really understood life, sleeping well is the first thing he mentions.

The Confucians connect a bad night with how one lives by day. Confucius observed that a person with nothing to hide is at ease, while someone forever calculating gain and loss is always on edge.

More than a thousand years later Wang Yangming made the point more concrete. A student told him that his mind was settled while he sat quietly and scattered as soon as anything happened. Wang answered that a calm grown only in quiet cannot be relied on.

Chan puts it most bluntly. Two monks were arguing over whether the wind was moving or the banner was moving. Huineng said: neither.

The four disagree on a good deal. The physicians say adjust the body first; the Daoists say do less; the Confucians say the trouble starts in the daytime; Chan says that even the thought "I must calm down" is the mind moving. On one thing they agree: none of them tells you to lie in bed and try harder to sleep.

2What the research says now

The central finding of modern insomnia research is close to that sentence in bold. In 2002 the psychologist Allison Harvey proposed a cognitive model: people with chronic insomnia worry at night about not sleeping and monitor themselves by day for signs of tiredness, and the worry and monitoring themselves keep them alert. In 2006 Colin Espie and colleagues went further. Sleep is automatic, they argued, and it is disrupted the moment a person starts attending to it, intending it and putting effort into it. They named the route "attention, intention, effort".

The lover in the Songs who seeks and cannot get, and the patient whose effort to sleep keeps sleep away, are three thousand years apart and share one structure. Here Laozi's 無為 wuwei, "not forcing", stops being mystical: some things can only be allowed to happen, never made to happen.

Research also has what the ancients lacked. First, it can test which practices actually work. The 2016 guideline of the American College of Physicians recommends cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia as the first treatment for chronic insomnia in adults, ahead of sleeping pills. One part of that therapy, stimulus control, says: if you cannot sleep, get up, and come back when you are sleepy; do not lie there forcing it. Second, it does not put everything down to the mind. Sleep apnoea, thyroid trouble, side effects of medication and depression all disturb sleep, and those call for tests, not self-cultivation.

Traditional body practices have been put through controlled trials as well. In 2014 Michael Irwin and colleagues compared cognitive behavioural therapy and tai chi in older adults with insomnia: both did better than a health-education class, and the behavioural therapy did best. A 2021 trial in Hong Kong found tai chi improved insomnia in older people about as much as conventional exercise. Read plainly: tai chi helps, and nothing shows it is more special than other exercise.

3What to take away

  • When you cannot sleep, recognise the seeking. The more you demand sleep now, the less it comes; on this the ancients and the researchers agree.
  • Getting up beats lying there. Huineng's line can be used this way: leave the wind and the banner alone, admit that the mind is moving, get out of bed, and wait for sleepiness to return by itself.
  • Settle the day in the daytime. Confucius's remark is a reminder that what surfaces at night is mostly the gain and loss you did not put down by day. Writing tomorrow's list before bed moves it from the mind to the page.
  • Do not eat late or heavily. "When the stomach is out of harmony one cannot lie easy" is the easiest line on this page to act on.
  • If you sleep badly three or more nights a week for three months, or cannot get through the day, see a doctor. It is not a failure of character.

To follow any one voice further: the Dao De Jing on not forcing, the Analects on how to live by day, the Platform Sutra on the moving mind, and the physicians through the gate.

    Hong Kong trial: tai chi and conventional exercise improved insomnia in older adults to a similar degree.
