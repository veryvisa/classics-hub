Chan Buddhism · Tang

The Platform Sutra of the Sixth Patriarch

A young man selling firewood in the far south, unable to read, overhears someone reciting the Diamond Sutra at an inn, understands something on the spot, takes leave of his mother and travels north to seek the teaching. The monastery sets him to pounding rice. Eight months later he asks someone to write his verse on a wall; at midnight the master hands him the robe and bowl and tells him to flee. This is Huineng's own account at the opening of the Platform Sutra, and one of the best-known underdog stories in China.

What kind of book this is

The Platform Sutra records sermons given by Huineng (638–713) at Dafan Monastery in Shaozhou, set down by his disciple Fahai and expanded by later hands. In Buddhism only the words of a buddha are called a sutra; this is the one work spoken by a Chinese monk to bear that title.

Its central claim is a single one: everyone already has a pure, awakened nature, and practice is not acquiring something from outside but recognising what was there from the start. So there is no need to leave home, to be literate, or to sit for years; and for the same reason no one has an excuse.

Read these lines first

This is Huineng's reply to a verse by the senior monk Shenxiu. Shenxiu said the mind is like a mirror that must be wiped constantly; Huineng said there is no need to posit a mirror at all. One side stood for gradual cultivation, the other for sudden awakening, and the difference became the emblem of the northern and southern schools of Chan.

The difference between an ordinary person and a buddha lies not in status but in the present thought. It is the most hopeful line in the book and the one that leaves the least room to hide.

The question of versions

The version usually read was edited by the monk Zongbao in 1291 and runs to more than twenty thousand characters. Manuscripts found at Dunhuang in the early twentieth century have only about twelve thousand, were copied around the eighth or ninth century, and are far plainer; in them the third line of the famous verse reads "buddha-nature is always pure" rather than "from the beginning there is not a single thing".

On the Dunhuang evidence Hu Shi argued that the substance of the Platform Sutra came from Huineng's disciple Shenhui, who shaped the figure of the "Sixth Patriarch" in a fight over legitimacy. Few scholars now accept this in full, but all agree that the text grew over a century or two and that the details of Huineng's life were worked up by later hands. It can be read as a tradition's account of its own origins.

How it stands with the others

Huineng says one's nature is "originally complete"; Mencius, a thousand years earlier, said everyone has the sprouts of goodness. The resemblance is no accident. Chan took root in China because it joined the Confucian confidence in human nature to the Daoist distrust of language. "Not relying on words" and Laozi's "the way that can be spoken of is not the constant Way" express the same wariness; see the Dao De Jing.

In turn Chan provoked the Confucians. Those of the Song and Ming had to answer: if the mind is pure to begin with, what are study and ritual for? Zhu Xi answered that one must learn thing by thing; Wang Yangming's answer came much closer to Chan, and he was accused of being "Confucian outside, Buddhist inside"; see the Chuanxilu.

The line can be read as the Confucian reply to Chan. Huineng said one can practise at home; Wang Yangming goes a step further and says that only what is cultivated in the handling of affairs counts.

And today's mindfulness

The mindfulness now taught in hospitals and companies descends mainly from Theravada insight practice and Japanese Zen, reworked by Jon Kabat-Zinn and others. It is a distant cousin of the Platform Sutra: both speak of watching thoughts without being carried off by them. But Huineng explicitly rejected equating Chan with sitting still and emptying the mind; he called that a sickness, not Chan. Reading the Platform Sutra as a relaxation manual misses its sharpest edge.

Translations

The most influential scholarly translation is Philip Yampolsky's of 1967, from the Dunhuang text; John McRae translated the Zongbao version in 2000. Both are in copyright. The English here is our own; quotations follow the Zongbao version, with notes where the Dunhuang text differs substantially.

Where else this book is quoted

  • Awake at three, and the mind will not stop 不是風動，不是幡動，仁者心動。
  • How does a restless mind settle? 不是風動，不是幡動，仁者心動。
  • I know what is right. Why can't I do it? 前念迷即凡夫，後念悟即佛。
  • How should people live together, and how should they be led? 若欲修行，在家亦得，不由在寺。
