Dao De Jing (Tao Te Ching)

One manager checks everything and the team grows passive. Another seems to do very little and things run smoothly. The eighty-one chapters of the Dao De Jing circle around this one observation: many things get worse the harder you push, and doing less takes far more skill than doing more.

What kind of book this is

The received text runs to about five thousand characters in eighty-one chapters; later readers called the first thirty-seven the "classic of the Way" and the remaining forty-four the "classic of virtue". Tradition makes its author, Laozi, an elder contemporary of Confucius, but the text was fixed much later, and most scholars now think it took shape gradually around the middle of the Warring States period.

Two excavations in the twentieth century rewrote its history. In 1973 two silk copies came out of a Han tomb at Mawangdui, Changsha, with the "virtue" half placed before the "Way" half, the reverse of the received order. In 1993 bamboo slips from a Chu tomb at Guodian, Hubei, closed around 300 BCE, turned out to hold only about two fifths of the received text and to lack the sharp attacks on Confucian benevolence and rightness found in later versions. The book grew in layers, and Daoists and Confucians were not at first as opposed as they later became.

Read these lines first

The opening line is a warning to the reader that everything that follows is only a finger pointing. Language cuts the world into pieces, and what Laozi wants to talk about comes before the cutting.

Water is the book's master image: it goes to the low places, flows around what blocks it, and in the end nothing stops it.

Whatever reaches its peak turns back. So Laozi advises against standing on the peak and recommends the position that still has room.

The word most often misread: wuwei

無為 wuwei does not mean doing nothing. Chapter 64 is explicit: deal with things while they are small, "act on it before it exists, order it before there is disorder". What Laozi objects to is forcing things against their grain, and action performed for show. "Non-action", the usual English rendering, misleads; "not forcing" is closer. The sinologist Edward Slingerland translates it as "effortless action" and reads it through cognitive science: actions practised until they need no thought are often performed better than actions under deliberate control.

How it stands with the others

Towards the Confucians, the received Laozi is a critic: benevolence and rightness are preached only once the great Way has been abandoned, and ritual is a sign that loyalty and trust have worn thin. Yet both are answering one question, what to do when the world is in disorder. Confucius says educate people; Laozi says stop meddling. The question map sets the two lines side by side.

For the physicians Laozi is a source. When the Huangdi Neijing speaks of calm emptiness and of treating what is not yet ill, it is using nearly his words; see the gate. And for later Chan, Laozi and Zhuangzi supplied the first vocabulary for translating Buddhist texts.

Translations

The Dao De Jing is among the most translated of all Chinese books. James Legge's version of 1891 is in the public domain and free to read, though its style is Victorian. Modern translations each make their own trade-offs; important ones include D. C. Lau (1963), Robert Henricks's two versions based on the silk and bamboo manuscripts, and the philosophical translation by Roger Ames and David Hall (2003). The English on this site is our own, aiming at clear sense rather than poetry.
